## Introduction

Ask Pythia-12B three questions of identical structure, and watch it fail three different ways. Why is an image without alt text not accessible? "It does not have a text alternative" — correct, and substantively so: the answer generalizes from the missing attribute to the functional category it serves, which is concept-work, not restatement. Why is a long navigation menu without a skip link not accessible? "Because of the following error: The page you are trying to reach is not available in the current context" — a fluent 404 template, confidently wrong. Why is a div with an onclick handler not accessible? "[VERBATIM QUOTE — tangent battery, div/onclick at 12B; CC: pull exact text from committed responses]" — an answer imported wholesale from a different domain. Same model, same prompt shape. The difference tracks corpus frequency. Alt text appears roughly 23,000 times as a compound in the model's training corpus; skip link, 662; the third question names no rare compound at all, and the answer arrives from somewhere else entirely. This paper asks what corpus frequency predicts, how far that prediction reaches inside the model, and what it does not explain.

Digital accessibility is an unusually good test domain for these questions, for reasons that have nothing to do with accessibility in particular. Its concepts are concrete enough to code deterministically — a definition of "skip link" is right or wrong by a practitioner's standard, not a matter of taste — and rare enough in web text to expose frequency effects that broad-capability benchmarks average away. The mechanism under study, however, is about words and their documentation density; accessibility is where we measured it, not what it is about.

The paper's claims, their evidence, and their independent validation status are summarized in Table 1, in the order the paper develops them.

Before submission, we ran the paper's claim structure through a blinded external-validation study as a reviewer pre-mortem: independent analyst sessions received the repository's data artifacts under ciphered names — no prose, no findings documents — and were asked what the data supports. Four of the five pre-registered claims above were independently recovered from artifacts alone, against recovery tiers frozen before the study ran. The fifth, registered in advance as the long shot, was not recovered as stated; the recovery attempt localized the disagreement to a measurement pathway, and the narrowed claim — with the audit that narrowing produced — is Section 6. The registration, scoring, and unblinding record are in the public repository.

The paper makes five contributions, in the order of Table 1's rows. First, we establish the declarative-evaluative gap behaviorally: across six Pythia scales and four GPT-2 scales, models that correctly define accessibility concepts fail to apply them, with the gap widening with scale in GPT-2 and, in Pythia, closing at 12B only because declarative knowledge regresses to meet evaluative failure — convergence from the wrong side. Second, we audit this program's own best mechanism candidate and demote it: sustained late-layer attention binding tracks emergence but is a correlate, not a cause — the binding heads are attention sinks and positional structure, the signal is not domain-specific, the most selective head is causally inert, and the binding survives intact at the scale where generation collapses. Third, we show corpus frequency predicts the failure structure: across 49 compounds, Spearman correlations of 0.57 (Pythia) and 0.51 (GPT-2) against a pre-registered threshold of 0.4, surviving confound controls, with the correct continuation's rank at the decision point tracking corpus counts directly — the knowledge is present and outranked, not absent. Fourth, we show the failure is confident: the entropy penalty for wrong domain answers shrinks toward zero past the emergence threshold, so that the largest models are barely more uncertain when wrong in-domain than when right on matched controls, which we state as a design constraint for any tool built on such models. Fifth, a correction we caught in our own work generalizes into a methods contribution: a common normalization-skipping measurement shortcut severs the one term in the output computation where these models store their frequency prior — a bias vector correlated with corpus frequency at ρ up to 0.78 — and autoregressive rollouts through that shortcut fabricate trajectories within seven steps at every scale we tested. The retraction, the pre-registered gate that caught it, and the mechanism behind it are all in the record.

We are equally explicit about what this paper does not claim. Frequency predicts; corpus-level causality requires intervention on training data and is future work. All results are on open-weight base models whose training corpora can be audited; whether they survive instruction tuning is unknown. And the mechanistic finding is largely negative — attention binding marks emergence without implementing it, and the failure does not condense into a findable circuit — a vacancy we document with controls at three levels and regard as informative: the knowledge in this frequency regime behaves like a statistical property of distributed weights, not like localized machinery.

The paper proceeds as interleaved investigations, each stating its method with its result: the behavioral gap (Section 2), the binding audit (Section 3), frequency and the failure structure it predicts (Section 4), confidence and fluent wrongness (Section 5), the measurement-pathway correction (Section 6), related work (Section 7), and discussion (Section 8). Every accuracy judgment is produced by deterministic, version-controlled criteria; every threshold in the paper was committed to the repository before its result was computed; and one exhibit that failed those standards is documented rather than absent.
